Why is my dog shedding so much?

A plain guide to the hair growth cycle, the spring and fall shed and the signs that mean it is time for a vet visit.

Written by the pawIQ team · Last updated November 16, 2026 · 5 min read

Dogs normally shed hair in early spring and early fall, and changes in temperature or daylight can bring on a shed too.1 Shedding is a reason to see your vet when it comes with patches of hair loss, a matching pattern of hair loss on both sides of the body, itching or skin changes.2

The hair growth cycle and shedding

Dog shedding is tied to a hair growth cycle, and dogs normally shed in response to changes in temperature and day length.3 The Merck Veterinary Manual names 3 stages: a growing stage (anagen), a transitional stage (catagen) and a resting stage (telogen), when the hair is mature.3

The coat has real work to do. It protects the skin from physical damage and ultraviolet light, and it helps regulate body temperature.1 That job changes with the weather, which is why the coat changes too.

How season and coat type affect shedding

Season, temperature, daylight and the dog's own coat type all play a part in how much a dog sheds.1,4

Dogs normally shed hair in the early spring and early fall, and they may also shed in response to changes in temperature or the amount of sunlight.1 Day length, called photoperiod, is one of the factors that control hair growth, along with nutrition and hormones.3

The coat itself changes with the seasons.1 The cold-weather coat of many dogs is longer and finer to hold in heat, while the warm-weather coat has shorter, thicker hairs and fewer secondary hairs so air can move through and cool the dog.1

Coat type matters too. The size, shape and length of hair are controlled by genetics.1 A Purdue University guide describes 8 coat types, from long and double (a short undercoat with a longer top coat) to curly, hairless and wire, which it describes as "stiff, coarse hair, low shedding."4

Different breeds have different coat types, and "some need more maintenance than others."4 Some breeds have double coats and shed heavily.4

Nutrition plays a part in coat health too.1 The growth of a dog's hair is affected by nutrition, hormones and the change of season,1 and good nutrition can help your dog have a healthier coat, so feed a good-quality dog food suited to your dog's life stage.4

When is dog shedding too much?

Shedding may be too much when it comes with patches of hair loss, hair loss that looks the same on both sides of the body, itching or skin changes.2 Hair loss can affect 1 spot or several areas, and inflammation, thickened skin, color change, scaling and itching are common alongside it.2

Watch for these signs of hair loss:2

  • Patches of hair loss, in 1 spot or several
  • Hair loss that is symmetric, meaning a matching pattern on both sides of the body
  • Hair loss in an area your dog is scratching a lot
  • Skin changes such as inflammation, thickened skin, color change or scaling
  • Hair loss in a single spot where something rubs, such as under a poorly fitted collar
  • If you see any of these signs, have a vet find the cause, since hair loss is a sign and its underlying cause must be determined.2

Possible causes include infections, parasites such as fleas or mites, allergies and hormone problems such as an underactive thyroid.2 Temporary hair loss can occur during pregnancy or while nursing, and also several weeks after a severe illness or fever.2

Hair loss is a sign, not a diagnosis, so the first step is finding the cause. The vet exam for hair loss includes a detailed history, a physical exam and a note of the pattern of hair loss.2 They will also look at the hairs themselves to see whether they are falling out of the follicle or breaking off.2

Talk to your vet. If your dog's shedding changes suddenly, leaves bare patches or comes with scratching or skin changes, your vet can look at the pattern and find the cause. It is also a good visit to ask which brushing routine and tools suit your dog's coat.

How to manage shedding at home with brushing

Regular brushing helps with shedding at home, because it removes loose hairs and prevents mats.4 That goes for every coat. Purdue's coat care guide says that "regardless of breed, all dogs should be brushed regularly," and that brushing also helps keep the coat clean, removes dead skin cells and helps keep the coat moisturized by stimulating natural oils.4

How often depends on the coat. Here is a suggested schedule by coat type:4

How often to brush, by coat type4
Coat typeSuggested brushing
Long-haired and silkyDaily
Thick undercoat1 or 2 times a week, or more
Curly-haired2 to 3 times a week
Wavy, such as PoodlesDaily
Short-haired and smoothAbout once a week

Pick tools your dog is comfortable with, avoid anything that pulls hair, and use deshedding tools with care, since they can irritate or even cut the skin.4

When the spring or fall shed starts, this short checklist puts the steps in one place:

  1. Look over your dog's coat every day to see if it needs more grooming.4
  2. Add extra brushing sessions as needed, since dogs may need more brushing while they are shedding.4
  3. While you brush, check the skin for cuts, sores or bumps.4
  4. Catch mats while they are small, when they can often be removed or detangled by hand.4
  5. Scan for the warning signs in the list above: patches of hair loss, a matching pattern on both sides, redness or scaling.2

Mats need a gentle approach. Mats can be painful because they pull on the skin, and trying to cut them out yourself can lead to accidental cuts.4 Larger or tighter mats are a job for your veterinarian or a professional groomer, and a groomer may use electric clippers.4
