What Thanksgiving foods can dogs eat?

A little plain turkey is generally fine. Stuffing, gravy and pumpkin pie are better left on your plate. Here is what to share, what to skip and why.

Written by the pawIQ team · Last reviewed October 28, 2026 · 6 min read

Dogs can have a small portion of plain, boneless, skinless turkey, which the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) calls generally safe for pets. Stuffing, gravy, turkey skin and pumpkin pie are better kept off your dog's plate, because fatty foods can cause pancreatitis and some holiday ingredients are toxic to dogs.1

Here is what is fine to share, what to skip and what to do if your dog helps itself on Thanksgiving.

Can dogs eat turkey?

Yes. Plain, boneless and skinless turkey is generally safe for pets in small portions.1

The skin, fat, gravy and sauces are a different story. These are hard for pets to digest, and the AVMA warns that "sometimes even a small amount can cause pancreatitis."1 The FDA Center for Veterinary Medicine describes pancreatitis as "a potentially life-threatening and painful disease" that rich and fatty foods can cause.2

Are turkey bones safe for dogs?

No. Poultry bones can damage your pet's digestive tract, and bones can also cause choking or a blockage.1

Dogs are known for helping themselves to the turkey carcass, and bones can get stuck in a dog's esophagus, stomach or windpipe.3 If a bone lodges in the stomach or intestines, signs such as vomiting or not wanting to eat can take hours to days to show up.3 After dinner, put the carcass and bones in a tightly secured trash bag, inside a closed bin or behind a closed door.1

Can dogs eat stuffing?

It is best to skip it. Like gravy, fat and skin, dressing (another name for stuffing) is hard for pets to digest.1

If the recipe includes onion or garlic, that adds a separate risk. Dogs have been affected after eating raw, cooked, dehydrated or granulated onions and garlic, and compounds in these plants damage red blood cells.4 The Merck Veterinary Manual notes that poisoning is most commonly linked to concentrated forms such as dehydrated flakes, powders and dry onion soup mixes, and that signs can take several days to appear.4 Raisins carry their own risk, covered below.

Can dogs eat pumpkin pie?

No. Plain pumpkin and pumpkin pie are 2 different things. The AVMA says small pieces of pumpkin "can be a healthy snack for pets," but "pumpkin pie can contain ingredients that are toxic to animals," so keep the pie for people.1

Sugar-free desserts need extra care. Xylitol, a sweetener used in some sugar-free baked goods and gum, can be deadly if dogs or cats eat it.1 Check the package label for xylitol, and the FDA says to call your veterinarian immediately if your dog ate a product that contains it.3 Chocolate desserts are off the table as well, since unsweetened or baking chocolate is especially dangerous to pets.3

Which other Thanksgiving foods are risky for dogs?

Grapes, raisins, raw yeast dough and alcohol are all risky for dogs, and each causes a different problem.

Grapes and raisins have been linked to kidney failure in some dogs, and the effects of eating them can vary.5 Raw yeast dough keeps rising in the warm stomach, and the yeast produces alcohol as it grows.6,7 The AVMA warns that uncooked yeast dough can cause "painful gas and potentially dangerous bloating."1 Alcohol itself can cause serious problems for pets, and severe cases can lead to coma and death.3

What can you give your dog instead?

A few bites of plain, boneless, skinless turkey or a small piece of plain pumpkin are the simple options.1 If you want your dog to have a holiday treat of its own, make or buy a treat made just for them.1

Guests can be part of the plan, too. Warn guests, especially children, not to slip your dog a treat, and keep people food, leftovers and bones out of reach.2,3 If your dog is nervous when people visit, put it in another room or a crate with a favorite toy.1

What should you do if your dog eats something it shouldn't?

Call your veterinarian or a local veterinary emergency clinic right away.1 The FDA's Center for Veterinary Medicine advises asking for help "instead of waiting for serious symptoms to develop."2

You can also call 1 of these 2 poison hotlines, though a fee may apply:1

  • ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center: 888-426-4435
  • Pet Poison Helpline: 855-764-7661

Signs to watch for include sudden behavior changes, depression, discomfort or pain, vomiting and diarrhea.1 Keep the package or recipe handy so you can say exactly what your dog ate.

Talk to your vet. Every dog is different. If your dog has a health condition, a sensitive stomach or a special diet, ask your veterinarian which holiday foods, if any, are right to share.

Frequently asked questions.

Can dogs eat turkey skin?

It is best not to. The AVMA says skin, fat and gravy are hard for pets to digest, and even a small amount can cause pancreatitis.1

Can dogs eat gravy or other side dishes?

Skip the gravy, a rich food that is hard for pets to digest. Skip any side dish made with onion or garlic too, since dogs have been affected by both raw and cooked forms. Ask your vet before sharing other sides.1,4

Is plain pumpkin OK for dogs?

Yes, in small pieces, which can be a healthy snack for pets. Pumpkin pie is different and should stay on the people table.1

How soon do signs show up after a dog eats something risky?

It depends on the food. Xylitol signs can appear in as little as 20 minutes, according to the FDA,2 while signs from onion or garlic can take several days.4 That is why it is best to call your vet right away rather than wait.

Who do I call on Thanksgiving if my vet is closed?

Call a local veterinary emergency clinic, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center (888-426-4435) or the Pet Poison Helpline (855-764-7661). A fee may apply.1
