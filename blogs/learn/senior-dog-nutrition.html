What changes in a senior dog's nutrition?

As dogs age, many need fewer calories, food that is easier to digest and closer attention to weight. Here is what changes in calories, food type and weight checks, and when.

Written by the pawIQ team · Last reviewed November 9, 2026 · 5 min read

As a dog gets older, its metabolism slows, its muscle mass starts to decline, and its nutritional needs change.1 In practice, many senior dogs need fewer calories than younger dogs, foods that are more easily digested, and closer attention to weight and muscle.1,2,3

Here is when a dog counts as senior, what shifts in the bowl, and how to keep weight steady through the later years.

When is a dog considered a senior?

A dog's senior age depends on its size: the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) says experts suggest dogs be considered senior when they reach the last 25% of the estimated life span for their breed.2 Giant breeds get there soonest.2

Approximate senior age by size2
SizeWeightApproximate senior age
Small or toy breedsLess than 20 lb (9 kg)8 to 11 years
Medium breeds20 to 50 lb (9 to 23 kg)8 to 10 years
Large breeds50 to 90 lb (23 to 41 kg)8 to 9 years
Giant breedsMore than 90 lb (41 kg)6 to 7 years

For a simpler rule of thumb, senior years typically start around age 7 for dogs.1 Age itself is not a disease.2

How calorie needs change in senior dogs

Senior dogs often need fewer calories: an older dog frequently does not need to eat the same number of calories as a younger dog.3 Activity can change too, since pets can become less active with age.2

Cutting calories can be as simple as feeding less or moving to a lighter or lower-calorie food.3 Your vet can tell you which approach suits your dog.

Muscle changes as a dog ages

Muscle mass begins to decline as dogs age, and that is part of why their nutritional needs change.1

Your vet can track muscle, too: alongside a body condition score, vets can assess an older pet's muscle condition score (MCS).1 At each visit, ask whether your dog's muscle condition score has changed and whether its food still fits its needs.

Many senior dogs need a different food

Many senior dogs do need a different food: senior pets often need foods that are more easily digested and that provide different energy levels and ingredients.2 Senior pet foods are specially formulated to support aging bodies, with adjusted calorie levels and easily digestible ingredients.1

Plan a diet change with your vet, too. The University of Georgia College of Veterinary Medicine recommends talking with your vet before any sudden change to a pet's diet.1

Keeping a senior dog's weight steady

Keeping a senior dog's weight steady means counting treats and table scraps, not just meals: both add calories to the diet, and calories from treats should make up less than 10% of the diet.4 The feeding amount on the bag is a good starting point, but it is based on a dog's ideal weight, not its current weight.4

Weight matters in both directions. Gaining weight increases a senior dog's risk of health problems.2 On the other side, unintended weight loss may signal an underlying illness or muscle loss.1 If you notice any sudden change in your dog's weight, contact your veterinarian.2

During an exam, your vet feels the ribs to judge how much fat covers them, and at an ideal weight the ribs should be easily felt without pressing too firmly.4 The University of Illinois College of Veterinary Medicine explains that vets use a body condition score (BCS), based on a physical exam, to tell whether a dog is overweight, and small animal medicine commonly uses 2 versions: a 5-point scale and a 9-point scale.4 On the 9-point scale, 1 means very thin, 9 means very overweight, and an ideal score is 4.5 to 5.4

How often a senior dog should see the vet

Senior dogs should see the vet more often than younger dogs: senior pets need to see a veterinarian 2 times a year or more.2

Exams and lab work every 6 to 12 months are also recommended for older dogs.3 Once your vet evaluates your dog's condition, they can recommend diet and other changes.2

Senior dogs still need low-impact exercise

Exercise still matters for senior dogs: older dogs may need fewer calories, but that does not mean they should live a sedentary life.3 Appropriate exercise helps senior pets stay healthier and more mobile.2

If running or jumping has become too hard, a senior dog can still benefit from walking, swimming or playing.3 Short walks, puzzle toys and low-impact play keep a dog moving without overexertion.1
