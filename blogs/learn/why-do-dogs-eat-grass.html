Why do dogs eat grass?

Many dogs eat grass, and it is usually normal. Here is what veterinary research says about why, when to call your vet, and how to keep lawn snacking safe.

Written by the pawIQ team · Last reviewed October 28, 2026 · 5 min read

Dogs eat grass for reasons researchers have not fully pinned down, but it is very common and generally considered normal dog behavior. In a large owner survey, most plant-eating dogs rarely seemed sick before or after, so watch for a sudden change in the habit and what is on the grass.

How common is grass eating in dogs?

Grass eating is very common in dogs. In a study from a veterinary teaching hospital, 79% of 47 owners whose dogs had daily access to plants said their dog had eaten grass or other plants.1

The same researchers then ran an internet survey of owners of plant-eating dogs and analyzed 1,571 responses. Overall, 68% of those dogs ate plants daily or weekly, and grass was the plant owners saw their dog eat most often.1

Is it normal for dogs to eat grass?

Yes, eating grass is normal for most dogs. The study's authors concluded that "plant eating is a normal behaviour of domestic dogs."1

Sex, reproductive status, breed group and diet type showed no clear link to how often dogs ate plants. Younger dogs ate plants more often than older dogs.1

A University of Wisconsin-Madison School of Veterinary Medicine article agrees: if your dog commonly eats grass, it is not an immediate cause for alarm.2

Do dogs eat grass to make themselves throw up?

Most dogs probably do not eat grass to make themselves throw up. In the survey, only 9% of dogs were reported to frequently seem ill before eating plants, and only 22% to frequently vomit afterward.1

Nausea may be a reason some dogs eat grass, but not always, according to the University of Wisconsin-Madison article.2 Cornell's Riney Canine Health Center describes infrequent vomiting, such as after eating grass, as likely an isolated incident.3

What are the main reasons dogs eat grass?

The exact reason dogs eat grass is not clearly known.2 Veterinary sources point to 3 explanations:

  • Normal scavenging: dogs are scavengers, and trying unusual things is common for them.2
  • An inherited habit: the survey authors suggested plant eating may reflect a predisposition passed down from wild canid ancestors, possibly as a way to expel intestinal parasites. They noted that controlled studies could test this idea.1
  • An upset stomach, sometimes: nausea may play a part for some dogs, though few grass eaters are reported to frequently seem ill first.1,2

When should you call your vet about grass eating?

Call your vet when grass eating is new for your dog or comes with other changes. A clinical instructor at UW-Madison put it this way: "If it's a new behavior, it may indicate a change in your dog's health." Look for other changes in behavior or daily habits, including any recent change in diet.2

Vomiting is the other thing to watch. Cornell's Riney Canine Health Center advises contacting your vet if you see:3

  • Blood in the vomit, or material that looks like coffee grounds
  • Lethargy
  • Vomiting that lasts more than 24 hours
  • A painful belly or a fever

Cornell also notes that vomiting as often as once a week may indicate a disease, so a regular pattern is worth a vet visit.3

Are lawn chemicals dangerous if my dog eats grass?

Some lawn chemicals are dangerous to dogs, so it pays to know what has been applied. A UW-Madison veterinary article explains that fertilizers are spread in dilute amounts but can cause vomiting and bloating if a dog eats a large quantity. Most herbicides and fungicides are heavily diluted too, and the most likely problem if a dog eats them is vomiting.4

Pesticides are the bigger concern. The same article says pesticides are generally toxic to pets, and insecticides affect the nervous system of dogs and need immediate veterinary care.4

A few habits lower the risk:

  • Keep your dog off treated areas until the lawn has been thoroughly watered and allowed to fully dry.5
  • If you use a lawn care service, tell them you have pets.5
  • Use pet-safe lawn and garden products when possible.5
  • Store all lawn and garden products out of reach or locked away.4

Which plants should dogs not eat?

Some plants and fungi around the home can be toxic. Texas A&M's College of Veterinary Medicine lists sago palms and rose laurel among non-garden toxic plants to watch for, and advises keeping pets away from mushrooms and compost piles.5

The same source suggests using a phone app to identify the plants in your yard, supervising pets outside, and not letting them drink from ponds or standing water, where blue-green algae can grow.5

What should you do if your dog eats something toxic?

If your dog eats something toxic, act right away. Texas A&M advises removing your pet from the source, then calling your veterinarian, an emergency veterinary hospital or a pet poison helpline immediately. Do not try to treat your dog at home unless a veterinarian tells you to.5

Bring the plant sample or product label if you can.5 If it was a lawn or garden product, take a photo of the packaging so you can tell a poison center the active ingredient.4

Talk to your vet. If your dog suddenly starts eating grass, or vomits often, check in with your veterinarian. They can look at the whole picture, including diet and daily routine.

Frequently asked questions.

Is it bad if my dog eats grass every day?

Not necessarily. In a large owner survey, 68% of plant-eating dogs ate plants daily or weekly, and the researchers concluded plant eating is normal dog behavior. If daily grass eating is new for your dog, or comes with vomiting or other changes, call your vet.

Should I stop my dog from eating grass?

If your dog commonly eats grass, it is not an immediate cause for alarm, according to UW-Madison's veterinary school. Do keep your dog off treated lawns until they have been thoroughly watered and allowed to fully dry, and away from toxic plants and mushrooms.

Why does my dog eat grass and then throw up?

Nausea may be part of it for some dogs, but in a large owner survey, 78% of owners said their dog rarely vomited after eating plants. Call your vet if vomiting lasts more than 24 hours, contains blood, or comes with lethargy, belly pain or fever.

Do younger dogs eat grass and other plants more often?

Yes, based on owner reports. In the survey, younger dogs ate plants more often than older dogs and were less likely to seem ill beforehand or vomit afterward.
