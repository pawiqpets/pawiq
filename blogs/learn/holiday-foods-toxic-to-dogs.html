What Christmas foods can't dogs eat?

December means cookie tins on the coffee table and plates left on the counter. Here is what to keep out of reach this holiday season, and who to call if your dog gets there first.

Written by the pawIQ team · Last reviewed November 11, 2026 · 7 min read

The main Christmas and holiday foods dogs can't eat are chocolate, xylitol (a sweetener in sugar-free products), grapes and raisins, macadamia nuts, alcohol and raw yeast dough.1,2 Rich leftovers like turkey, ham and gravy, and dishes made with onion or garlic, are risky too.2,3 If your dog eats any of them, get help from a veterinarian right away.2

Why chocolate is dangerous for dogs

Chocolate is dangerous for dogs because it contains theobromine and caffeine, and dark and baking chocolate are especially risky.1,4 The Merck Veterinary Manual ranks dry cocoa powder and unsweetened chocolate highest, followed further down by semisweet and sweet dark chocolate, then milk chocolate.1 White chocolate contains insignificant amounts of theobromine and caffeine, but the simplest rule is still to keep chocolate away from your dog.1,4 Dogs are the pets most often poisoned by chocolate, because they tend to eat anything and chocolate is readily available.1 How sick a dog gets varies with its sensitivity, and 1 ounce of milk chocolate per pound of body weight (about 62 g per kg) is a potentially fatal amount.1

A dog may become very thirsty, vomit, have diarrhea and become restless, and signs can progress to hyperactivity, lack of coordination, tremors and seizures.1 Signs usually begin within 6 to 12 hours.1 If you think your dog ate chocolate, call your vet straight away.2

Xylitol in sugar-free treats

Some sugar-free products contain xylitol, a sweetener that can be toxic to dogs.1,2 Xylitol is a sugar alcohol used to sweeten sugar-free products such as gums, candies, peanut and nut butters and baked goods.1

In dogs, xylitol can cause a rapid, severe drop in blood sugar.1 At high doses, it can also cause liver injury or failure.1

Signs of low blood sugar can appear within 30 minutes and include vomiting, weakness and lack of coordination.1 Because blood sugar can drop fast, trying to make your dog vomit at home is not recommended.1 Call your vet right away.

Grapes, raisins and currants, including Christmas cake

Grapes, raisins and Zante currants have been linked to poisoning in dogs, and grapes and raisins have led to kidney failure in some dogs.1,5

The effects vary from dog to dog. Tartaric acid, a proposed cause, varies between fruits, and Cornell University's College of Veterinary Medicine says "the amount that causes harm is unknown and cannot be predicted."1,5,6 Baked goods with raisins, such as Christmas cake, are worth flagging to guests.4

Most affected dogs vomit or have diarrhea within 6 to 12 hours.5 Treat any amount as an emergency, and contact your veterinarian or a pet poison control center right away.6

Macadamia nuts and the signs to watch for

Macadamia nuts can cause weakness, vomiting and tremors in dogs, the only species in which illness has been reported.1,7

Within 12 hours, affected dogs may vomit and become weak, depressed and uncoordinated, and tremors and a fever can also appear.1 Signs generally resolve within 12 to 48 hours.1,7 Call your vet right away if your dog eats macadamia nuts, even if the signs look mild.

Alcohol and New Year's Eve drinks

Alcoholic drinks can cause neurologic signs in dogs, including coma.2 On New Year's Eve, collect glasses as soon as guests put them down.

How raw bread dough harms dogs

Raw yeast dough keeps expanding inside a dog's stomach and produces alcohol as it ferments.1 Merck describes the warm, moist stomach as "an efficient incubator for yeast," and the ethanol made during fermentation is absorbed into the bloodstream.1

The dough can bloat the stomach, and in some dog breeds it can cause the digestive tract to twist.1 The alcohol and other effects can end in a coma or even death.4

Can dogs eat Christmas dinner?

Christmas dinner is best kept on your own plate, because rich leftovers like turkey, ham and gravy, bones and dishes made with onion or garlic can all make dogs sick.2,3 Our guide on what Thanksgiving foods can dogs eat covers turkey, stuffing and pie, and the same thinking applies in December.

Holiday treats of turkey, ham, gravy and cookies can lead to vomiting and diarrhea, or to pancreatitis, which can be life threatening.2 Poultry bones can also get lodged in your dog's food pipe.2 It is best to keep your dog on its regular diet.2

Dishes seasoned with onion or garlic are worth skipping too. Dogs have been affected after eating raw, cooked, dried or powdered onions and garlic, which damage red blood cells.3 Signs can take several days to appear.3

What to do if your dog eats something toxic

If your dog eats something toxic, call your own vet or your local emergency vet right away.2,4 Do not wait to see if signs appear, because some foods cause trouble quickly and others take days.1,3

Leave any treatment to your vet rather than trying something at home. Keep the package, label or recipe handy so you can say exactly what your dog ate and roughly how much. Looking up your nearest 24-hour emergency vet before the holidays start saves time later.

You can also call 1 of 2 animal poison control lines. Both charge, or may charge, a consultation fee. Here is what each service's own website says:8,9

  • ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center, (888) 426-4435: its website says a consultation fee may apply but does not list the amount.8
  • Pet Poison Helpline, (855) 764-7661: its website says a $99 fee applies per incident, and all follow-up consultations are included.9

Keeping your dog safe at holiday gatherings

2 simple steps help keep your dog safe at holiday gatherings: keep food out of reach and tell guests the plan.4,6

  • Store food securely where your dog cannot reach it.6
  • Keep alcoholic drinks and baking supplies up high.4
  • After the meal, dispose of bones carefully so your dog cannot get to them.2
  • Tell guests which foods are off limits, especially baked goods with raisins.4
  • Give your dog a safe, quiet room to retreat to when the house gets busy.4

Talk to your vet. Your veterinarian knows your dog's health history and is the best person to call about anything your dog ate. Ask them which holiday treats are a good fit for your dog.
