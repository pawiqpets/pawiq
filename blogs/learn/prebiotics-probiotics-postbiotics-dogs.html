What's the difference between prebiotics, probiotics and postbiotics for dogs?

Prebiotics, probiotics and postbiotics sound alike but are not the same. Here is what each one is and what research reviews describe for each.

Written by the pawIQ team · Last updated November 27, 2026 · 5 min read

Probiotics add live microbes, prebiotics are food for certain microbes already living in the body, and postbiotics are microbial parts or whole microbes that are no longer alive.1,2,3 By its expert definition, each one must provide a health benefit to the one that takes it, called the host.1,2,3

Why a dog's gut microbes matter

A dog's gut microbes matter because they play a key role in the dog's health: they help regulate digestion, immune responses (the body's defenses) and energy metabolism (how the body turns food into energy).4 Certain microbes, tiny living things such as bacteria, become established in a dog's intestines within a few hours after birth, and the Merck Veterinary Manual describes intestinal microbes as beneficial, in some cases aiding digestion.5

What is a probiotic?

A probiotic adds live microbes, given in an adequate amount to provide a health benefit. An expert panel of the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP) agreed this definition.1

What do probiotics and prebiotics do in the gut? A 2024 review of research in dogs says probiotics and prebiotics play roles in regulating intestinal health in 3 ways: shaping the mix of gut microbes, fortifying the gut lining (the barrier between the gut and the rest of the body) and enhancing the gut's immune defenses.6

The same review says probiotics can interact with other gut microbes by competing for nutrients and by releasing substances that act on other microbes, to support a stable microbe mix.6 The review describes trials in dogs that indicated probiotics can increase the amount of IgA in the gut, an antibody that helps shield the gut lining.6

Prebiotics are food for microbes already in the body

A prebiotic is food for certain microbes already living in the body, and it has to provide a health benefit.2 This definition applies to animals as well as people.2 The same 2024 review of research in dogs describes prebiotics, together with probiotics, as playing roles in those 3 ways.6 The review reports that, in dogs, adding the prebiotic FOS (fructooligosaccharides) to the diet increased the number of probiotic bacteria (bacteria often used as probiotics), such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus.6

The review also describes short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate, compounds made by gut microbes, which can increase under the action of probiotics and prebiotics and which can help steer immune cells and IgA responses in the gut.6

What is a postbiotic?

A postbiotic is made of microbial parts, or whole microbes, that are no longer alive, sometimes with compounds the microbes made.3 Under the expert definition, effective postbiotics contain inactivated microbial cells or their parts that contribute to observed health benefits.3

Postbiotics can come from yeasts as well as bacteria.7 A 2026 review of yeast probiotics and postbiotics, not specific to dogs, discusses biologically active substances from living or inactivated yeast and describes their roles in adjusting immune responses (immune modulation) and in supporting the body's lining layers.7

Frequently asked questions.

Are prebiotics and probiotics the same thing?

No, prebiotics and probiotics are different. Probiotics are live microbes, while prebiotics are substances that certain microbes already living in the body use as food.1,2

Are postbiotics alive?

Postbiotics are not alive, but under the expert definition effective postbiotics contain inactivated microbial cells or their parts that contribute to observed health benefits.3 A 2026 review, not specific to dogs, describes roles for biologically active substances from living or inactivated yeast in adjusting immune responses and supporting the body's lining layers.7

Do these definitions apply to dogs?

The prebiotic definition applies to animals as well as people.2 The probiotic and postbiotic definitions describe a health benefit for "the host" (the one that takes it) without naming a species.1,3 The 2024 review looks at probiotics and prebiotics in dogs.6
