What's the difference between prebiotics, probiotics and postbiotics for dogs?

Three words that sound alike and mean different things. Here is what each one is and what published research in dogs has found.

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read

Probiotics are live microorganisms, prebiotics are substances that a dog's own resident microbes selectively use, and postbiotics are microorganisms that have been deliberately inactivated, or their components. Each term has a formal definition from an international panel of scientists.

Here is what each term means, how they compare, and what published research in dogs has found.

What is a probiotic?

A probiotic is defined as "live microorganisms which when administered in adequate amounts confer a health benefit on the host." That wording comes from the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations and the World Health Organization, and an expert panel from the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP) reaffirmed it in 2014.

Two parts of that definition matter. The microbes have to be alive, and they have to be given in adequate amounts. Counts of live bacteria are measured in colony forming units, or CFU, which is why you see that abbreviation on probiotic labels.

A 2016 review of the canine research notes that the specific effects of different strains, and of strain combinations, have not been evaluated sufficiently.

What is a prebiotic?

ISAPP's 2017 consensus defines a prebiotic as "a substrate that is selectively utilized by host microorganisms conferring a health benefit." Put simply, a prebiotic is something the body's own microbes use selectively, and it only counts as a prebiotic if a health benefit has been documented. The panel states that the definition also applies to prebiotics for animals.

Common examples include fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS) and longer-chain inulin, both of which are listed among the prebiotics used in canine research. Because the definition requires selective use by the body's own microbes and a documented benefit, not every fiber qualifies as a prebiotic.

What is a postbiotic?

Postbiotics have the newest definition of the three. In 2021, an ISAPP panel defined a postbiotic as a "preparation of inanimate microorganisms and/or their components that confers a health benefit on the host."

The panel drew a few clear lines. Postbiotics are deliberately inactivated microbial cells, with or without metabolites or cell components. Purified microbial metabolites on their own are not postbiotics. A postbiotic does not have to come from a probiotic. And the benefit must be confirmed in the target host, which the panel says can include companion animals.

In dog research, yeast is a common source. A 2025 systematic review found that six of the 13 studies in live dogs used products made from Saccharomyces cerevisiae and all six were heat-inactivated.

What does the research in dogs actually show?

Less than you might expect. The 2016 canine review concluded that, despite widespread use of probiotics and prebiotics, scientific evidence of their beneficial effects in different conditions of the dog is scarce. It also noted that some promising lab results could not be repeated consistently in live animals, and that because probiotics are not usually defined as drugs, they do not have to go through a process proving they work.

Postbiotic research in dogs is younger still. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis found 13 studies in live dogs, and the outcomes it was able to pool showed no statistically significant differences between dogs given postbiotics and control groups. The authors point to small sample sizes, differences between strains and varied study designs as limits, and call for more research.

In short, the evidence in dogs is still early and often based on small studies.

Talk to your vet. Before you add any supplement to your dog's routine, check with your veterinarian, especially if your dog has a health condition, takes medication, or has had recent digestive changes.

What should you look for on a label?

Label accuracy has been a problem. In a 2011 study, researchers bought 25 probiotic products marketed for animals and tested what was inside. Of the 15 products that made a specific claim about the number of live organisms, only four met or exceeded it, and seven of the 22 products that named specific organisms misspelled at least one of them.

The study's authors advised veterinarians and owners to scrutinize commercial probiotics and ask for evidence of quality control and efficacy. Your veterinarian is a good person to ask which products they trust and why.

Frequently asked questions.

Are prebiotics and probiotics the same thing?

No. Probiotics are live microorganisms. Prebiotics are substances that a dog's own resident microbes selectively use, and to qualify, a health benefit must be documented.

Is a postbiotic just a dead probiotic?

Not exactly. Under the 2021 ISAPP definition, a postbiotic is a deliberately inactivated microorganism or its components, with a confirmed health benefit in the target species. It does not have to come from a probiotic, and purified microbial metabolites on their own do not count.

What does CFU mean on a dog probiotic?

CFU stands for colony forming units, the measure used to count live bacteria. Because a probiotic must be given in adequate amounts, the CFU count tells you how many live organisms the product claims to contain.

Is there strong evidence that probiotics, prebiotics or postbiotics work in dogs?

Not yet. Reviews of the canine research describe the evidence as scarce or limited, often from small studies. Ask your veterinarian whether a supplement makes sense for your dog.
