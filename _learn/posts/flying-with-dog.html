To fly with a dog, check your airline's pet rules before you book, see your vet within 10 days of the flight for the health certificate airlines generally require, and practice with the carrier.1,2 Your dog also comes out of the carrier for airport security.3

How do you fly with a dog, step by step?

Flying with a dog goes in this order: airline rules, booking, carrier practice, vet visit, then the day of the flight. The timeline below adds the timing the sources give, where they give one.

Flying with a dog, step by step
WhenWhat to do
Before you bookRead your airline's current pet policy for breed and size limits, weather and time-of-year rules, and charges for checked crates.1
When you bookReserve a space for your dog, ask about drop-off and pick-up, choose a nonstop flight, and avoid busy holiday and weekend travel.1,2
In the days beforeGet your dog used to the carrier or kennel, with a familiar toy or blanket inside, and check that tags and microchip details are current.1,3
Within 10 days of the flightSee your vet for a health exam and, if your airline requires one, a health certificate. For a flight to another state or country, you also need a certificate of veterinary inspection issued within 10 days before the flight.1,2
Day of the flightWalk your dog before the flight, and look up the pet relief areas at both airports.2,3
After landingCheck your dog's appetite, energy and behavior each day, and see a local vet about changes that worry you.1

Cabin and cargo hold travel follow different rules

A dog in the cabin rides in a carrier that fits under the seat on airlines that allow it, while a dog in the cargo hold travels in a pressurized space in a kennel that meets USDA standards.2 The U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT) says each airline sets its own pet policy, so check with the airline you plan to use.2 Some airlines may also charge a special fee or an excess baggage charge for a pet that travels with you.2

The hold has extra requirements, listed below, and your airline may add more.2,4

Cargo hold requirements and tips
ItemWhat the sources say
AgeThe dog must be at least 8 weeks old and fully weaned.4
PaperworkA health and acclimation certificate, signed by a licensed vet within 10 days of travel, should show the dog's readiness for travel and the acceptable travel temperature range.4
Drop-offA dog may not be brought to the airline more than 4 hours before departure, or 6 hours if arranged in advance.2
ColdAnimals may not be exposed to temperatures below 45°F (about 7°C) unless a vet's certificate says they are acclimated to lower temperatures.2
KennelThe kennel should be hard-sided and must have ventilation on 3 sides and the door, with "Live Animal" labels on the top and at least 1 side.4
Food and waterThe owner certifies the dog was offered food and water within 4 hours of delivery to the airport, with written instructions for the next 24 hours.4 DOT separately advises no solid food in the 6 hours before the flight.2

Short-nosed dogs face more risk on a flight

Short-nosed dogs such as Pugs, French Bulldogs and Boston Terriers face a higher risk of heat stroke and breathing problems when exposed to stress or extreme heat during air travel.1,4 These problems also occur in crossbreeds of these dogs.4 The American Veterinary Medical Association says that if the trip is optional, a pet sitter or a boarding facility your vet recommends is worth considering.1 If your dog stays home, see how to prepare your dog for a first boarding stay.

Talk to your vet. Before you book, think about whether flying is right for your dog, especially a short-nosed dog or one with illness, injury or temperament concerns, and talk to your vet about making the trip as safe and comfortable as possible.1

What happens at airport security when you fly with a dog?

At airport security, the Transportation Security Administration (TSA) has you take your dog out of the carrier for screening while the empty carrier goes through the X-ray.3 A dog never goes through the X-ray tunnel.3

  1. Bring your dog to the checkpoint in a hand-held carrier.3
  2. Take your dog out just before screening and put the empty carrier on the belt.3
  3. Carry your dog through the metal detector if possible, or walk the dog through on a leash, and follow the guidance of the officer.3
  4. Expect an officer to swab your hands for explosive residue.3
  5. Put your dog back in the carrier at the re-composure area, away from the checkpoint.3

If your dog might struggle or jump away, TSA says you can ask for screening in a private room, and you are escorted there with your dog still in the carrier.3

What to pack and which documents to bring

A packing list for a flight with a dog covers documents, ID, gear, food, medication and emergency contacts.1

  • Proof of vaccination, especially rabies, copies of relevant medical records and, if you cross a state or international border, a certificate of veterinary inspection.1
  • A collar with tags, microchip documentation, a travel tag (if possible) with where you are staying and a recent photo of your dog.1
  • Your leash, a portable kennel or carrier, and food and water bowls.1
  • Enough food for the entire trip, plus water in case none is available at a stop.1
  • Every medication your dog needs, since you should not count on getting refills along the way.1
  • A first aid kit and phone numbers for your vet, a national poison control hotline and a 24-hour emergency vet at your destination.1

Frequently asked questions.

Can any vet sign a certificate of veterinary inspection for a dog flying to another state or country?

No. A certificate of veterinary inspection must be signed by a federally accredited vet, and not every vet is one, so call ahead.1

How far ahead should you plan to fly with a dog?

For a flight to another state or country, preparations may need to start 6 months or more ahead, and some destinations also require a microchip, often of a certain type.1

What time of day is best to fly with a dog?

Early morning or late evening flights suit warm months, when temperatures are generally not as hot, and afternoon flights suit colder months, when temperatures are generally warmer.1
