Probiotics are live microorganisms, prebiotics are substances that a dog's own resident microbes selectively use, and postbiotics are microorganisms that have been deliberately inactivated, or their components. Scientists have agreed on a working definition for each one, summarized below.

What is a probiotic?

A probiotic is defined as "live microorganisms which when administered in adequate amounts confer a health benefit on the host." That wording comes from the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations and the World Health Organization. An expert panel from the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP) reaffirmed it in a 2014 consensus statement.

The key word is live. A probiotic is made of live microorganisms, which is what separates it from the other two terms.

What is a prebiotic?

ISAPP's 2017 consensus defines a prebiotic as "a substrate that is selectively utilized by host microorganisms conferring a health benefit." Put simply, a prebiotic is something the microbes already living in the body selectively use.

The panel states that its definition also applies to prebiotics for animals.

What is a postbiotic?

Postbiotics have the newest definition of the three. In 2021, an ISAPP panel defined a postbiotic as a "preparation of inanimate microorganisms and/or their components that confers a health benefit on the host."

A postbiotic is made from microorganisms that have been deliberately inactivated, or from their components. A postbiotic does not have to come from a probiotic, and the panel notes that the host can include companion animals. Researchers have studied inactivated forms of both bacteria and yeasts, such as the yeast Saccharomyces boulardii.

How do you spot each one on a label?

Look for each one by name on the label: the prebiotic ingredients, the probiotic species and the postbiotic source, such as a yeast extract. The three terms describe different things, so one does not stand in for another.

What should you ask your vet?

Your veterinarian knows your dog's history, diet and any medications, so they are the best person to help you choose. A few questions make that conversation quicker:

  • Which of the three fits my dog's age, size and routine?
  • Would a product that combines a prebiotic, a probiotic and a postbiotic suit my dog?
  • What is the easiest way to make a daily supplement part of my dog's routine?

Bring the product label with you. Seeing the named ingredients and amounts helps your vet give a specific answer.

Talk to your vet. Before you add any supplement to your dog's routine, check with your veterinarian, especially if your dog has a health condition or takes medication.

Frequently asked questions.

Are prebiotics and probiotics the same thing?

No. Probiotics are live microorganisms. Prebiotics are substances that a dog's own resident microbes selectively use.

Is a postbiotic just a dead probiotic?

Not exactly. Under the 2021 ISAPP definition, a postbiotic is a preparation of deliberately inactivated microorganisms or their components. It does not have to come from a probiotic.

Do the definitions apply to dogs?

Each definition is written around a host rather than people specifically. ISAPP's prebiotic consensus statement adds that the definition applies to animals, and its postbiotic consensus statement notes that the host can include companion animals.

Which of the three was defined most recently?

Postbiotics. An ISAPP panel published the current definition in 2021, after the 2014 probiotic and 2017 prebiotic consensus statements.
