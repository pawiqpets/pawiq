To calm a scared dog down, stay neutral, act as though nothing unusual is going on, and ask for a simple cue like "look" or "sit" that you can reward with a treat.1,2 Avoid punishment, because punishing a scared dog may strengthen its negative association with whatever scares it.2

A scared or anxious dog is the focus here, not an overexcited one. For other behavior concerns, owners should never hesitate to discuss them with their veterinarian.1

Signs of stress in dog body language

The most common sign of fear or anxiety in dogs is a change from normal behavior, such as freezing, seeking attention from the owner, shaking, trembling or whining.1 Watch for these body language signs too:3,4

  • Licking the lips, or yawning when not tired
  • Panting when not hot, or trembling when not cold
  • Ears pinned back and a tail tucked down
  • Turning the head away, or showing the whites of the eyes
  • Going still with a stiff body

A dog that leans back or avoids interaction is showing that it is not comfortable.1 Dogs may also give a shake, like shaking off water, to reduce tension or stress.4

Telling an eager dog from a stressed one

Some stress signs also show up in an eager dog, so read the whole body.3 Trembling and whining can mean fear, but they can also mean a dog is excited and eager to do something.3 A dog racing around wildly, back and forth or in circles, is usually playing, even with its tail tucked.3

A content, calm dog tends to show relaxed body language, such as squinting, a soft gaze, leaning on you, lying down or rolling over.4 A dog that is still and stiff while doing these things is not relaxed.3 Breed matters when you read ears and tails: German Shepherds often have upright ears and Huskies have curled tails, even when relaxed.4

How do I calm my dog down when it is scared?

Helping a scared dog includes staying neutral and not moving too quickly.1,2 On anxious behavior, Dr. Bonnie Beaver, a professor of behavior at the Texas A&M College of Veterinary Medicine & Biomedical Sciences, says: "Be neutral. Don't discourage it, don't punish it, but don't encourage it either."1 Early experiences and consistent responses can shape how dogs handle stressful situations over time.1 All family members should be consistent and treat the dog in the same manner.2

How you approach matters as well. Many dogs are frightened when people bend over them or reach for them.3 Approaching from the side and talking in a friendly way without looking straight at the dog can help avoid a fearful reaction.3 Don't try to teach a dog not to growl, because that can lead to a dog that snaps or bites with no vocal warning.3

Give your dog a safe spot and some space

Dogs often have a spot on or under furniture where they "hide in plain sight."3 When your dog goes there, leave it alone or call it to you rather than reaching in or pulling it out.3 Don't expose your dog to the full force of what it fears, since that can make the fear worse.2 If bath time is the trigger, see these tips on bathing a dog, including a nervous one.

How much daily activity experts recommend

For overall wellness, many experts recommend 40 to 45 minutes of daily activity for pets, for example 2 sessions of 20 minutes or 3 sessions of 15 minutes.5 A walk also gives a dog mental stimulation, with new things to smell, see and hear beyond the yard.6

When to get help with an anxious dog

Dog owners can ask for help when a dog's anxiety lasts longer or becomes more severe, and asking is fine even when the anxiety is mild.1 If a normally active, happy dog starts to hide, seems sluggish or eats less, see your veterinarian, since that change can point to a health problem.4 For fear of a specific trigger, getting a dog used to it slowly and gradually is generally recommended, and your vet can help you plan it.2

Frequently asked questions.

Should I comfort my dog when it is scared?

Petting or picking up a scared dog may enhance its reaction to what scares it, because the dog can see the comfort as positive reinforcement of fearful behavior.2

Should I keep my dog away from what scares it?

Initially, it is often best to avoid the things that trigger a fearful reaction, which may give you a chance to build trust without distraction.2

Talk to your vet. Bring up any new fear, nervousness or change in behavior at your dog's next visit, or sooner if it gets worse.1 A change in behavior can also point to a health problem.4
